From fields to colloid appearance

A scattering calculation begins with a particle and an incident field. A colloid photograph adds several further ingredients: the number of particles, the distance travelled through the suspension, the illumination and the collection of scattered light. The purpose of this page is to connect those steps. Otherwise it is easy to ask a correct calculation to predict the wrong observable.

For a first reading, follow T1–T6. Sections T7–T9 discuss interacting particles, inverse problems and material models. The prefix T numbers sections of this reference page; it is separate from the lesson numbers. Detailed equations and source tables are collected in the reference foldout at the end.

T1 · Specify the measurement

Consider a dilute suspension illuminated by a narrow beam. A detector placed behind the sample records the light that remains within its acceptance angle. A detector placed at 90° records a different part of the scattered field. An integrating instrument collects light over a much larger range of directions. These measurements can have different spectra even though the particles are identical.

Begin by identifying what the ordinate means. A field amplitude is complex and contains phase. An intensity is proportional to its squared magnitude. A cross section relates a removed or scattered power to the incident irradiance and has units of area. Optical density, in contrast, describes attenuation through a specified sample. Normalizing a curve to its own maximum removes information about absolute strength; it can be useful for comparing shapes, but cannot test a predicted concentration.

T2 · Compare the particle with the wavelength in its medium

The natural size variable is x = 2π nmeda / λ0, where a is the radius, λ0 the vacuum wavelength and nmed the refractive index of the transparent surrounding medium. A 100 nm diameter sphere in water at 500 nm has x ≈ 0.84. Its diameter alone would not tell us whether a small-particle approximation is adequate.

The second input is the relative complex refractive index, m = (n + iκ)/nmed. The Rayleigh approximation requires both x and |m|x to be small. A high-index particle can violate the second condition while satisfying the first. The exact Mie result in the app lets you examine the resulting error instead of adopting one diameter as a universal boundary.

Changing radius at fixed wavelength differs from sweeping wavelength for a fixed particle. In a wavelength sweep, n and κ also change. The B&H water examples in the Museum show the consequence particularly clearly. See B&H §5.1, pp. 130–135, for the small-sphere limit.

T3 · Find the field before calculating its power

Maxwell’s equations and the boundary conditions determine the fields inside and outside a sphere. Spherical vector waves provide a convenient basis: the labels electric and magnetic describe two families of solutions, and the multipole order gives their angular structure. They do not imply that the material must be magnetically permeable. A plane wave has a particular expansion in these waves; it does not excite an arbitrary combination.

Lessons 00 and 01 separate the basis functions from their excitation. In Radiation pattern, radius represents intensity. In On the unit sphere, the radius is fixed and arrows show the instantaneous field. The diagrams are two representations of a field, not two particle shapes. The actual scattered field is a coherent sum: add the complex amplitudes first, then square. Adding intensities would discard interference.

The series is truncated after a finite number of orders. Increasing the particle size generally requires more orders. A finer plotting mesh and a longer field expansion solve different numerical problems. Likewise, zooming a polar graph or the 3D pattern changes its display scale without altering the electromagnetic solution. B&H §§4.1–4.2, pp. 83–92, develop the basis and plane-wave expansion.

T4 · Account for extinction, scattering and absorption

Extinction is the power removed from the incident beam, expressed as a cross section. For a passive particle, Cext = Csca + Cabs. Scattering redistributes power into other directions; absorption transfers it to the material. A transmission spectrophotometer often calls their combined attenuation “absorbance”, even when scattering contributes appreciably.

The efficiency Q = C/(πa²) divides a cross section by the geometrical area. Q can exceed one. A wave interacts with a field extending beyond the geometrical silhouette, and extinction includes interference between the incident and forward-scattered fields. There is no conflict with energy conservation. For a chain, check whether the displayed efficiency is per sphere or for the complete assembly.

An angular pattern gives information that an integrated cross section cannot. Two particles can scatter the same total power but distribute it differently. At large x, a narrow forward lobe may dominate the total while a much weaker rainbow or interference fringe remains of interest. Use the complete angular graph to locate the feature, then magnify it. B&H §3.4, pp. 69–81, gives the definitions and power balance.

T5 · From one particle to a dispersion

For independent particles, the attenuation coefficient is μext = Np⟨Cext⟩. The number concentration Np and the path length L give an optical depth τ = μextL. The unscattered transmission is exp(−τ), and its decadic optical density is τ/ln(10). These expressions describe the direct beam; they do not imply that no light reaches a camera after scattering.

As a numerical example, 50 nm diameter spheres at a volume fraction of 1 ppm have a number concentration of about 1.5 × 10¹⁰ mL⁻¹. If their extinction cross section at a chosen wavelength is 5000 nm², a 1 cm path gives τ ≈ 0.76, direct transmission ≈ 47%, and optical density ≈ 0.33. This example specifies a cross section; it is not a claim that every 50 nm material has that value.

For a distribution, average cross sections over the number distribution and determine number concentration from the mean particle volume. Substituting the mean radius into a single-particle formula generally gives a different result. The larger particles can dominate scattering even when they are a small fraction of the count. A lognormal distribution is useful because sizes remain positive; its arithmetic mean, median and mode are different. The size sliders in the app specify arithmetic means and the spread is a coefficient of variation.

For core–shell particles, core radius and shell thickness can be distributed separately. The sampled outer radius is their sum. Broadening from size spread, material absorption, electron scattering and particle coupling should be examined separately before being combined. The Museum fits state which parameters are shared across a sample family. B&H §11.3, including Figs. 11.5–11.6 on pp. 297–298, illustrates the loss of fine extinction structure in a polydispersion.

T6 · Why a colloid has more than one colour

A transmission colour is obtained by weighting the source spectrum with the spectral transmission and the observer’s colour-matching functions. It therefore depends on the light source, concentration and path length as well as on the particle. Colour design uses a specified 1 cm transmission geometry. Changing the source can change the best design; matching three colour coordinates under one source does not guarantee a match under another.

A front-lit vial includes light scattered towards the observer, reflections from the glass and light arriving from the background. A back-lit vial is often dominated by transmitted light. Metal colloids can consequently show a transmission colour together with a different scattering colour near curved edges. A weakly absorbing dielectric suspension may look milky under front illumination yet transmit a warm tint when it preferentially removes shorter wavelengths from a beam. These are different observations of the same dispersion.

The Lightbox combines wavelength-dependent scattering and absorption with a glass container, liquid surface, source and backdrop. Its progressive preview estimates the appearance first and then refines the transport and glass features. The lighting is defined for comparison between samples; it is not a calibration of a particular camera. At appreciable optical depth, scattered photons can encounter more particles. The resulting multiple scattering can return light to the camera that the direct-beam exponential alone would count as lost.

What to compare in the Lightbox

Hold the container, source and lighting geometry fixed while changing the material or concentration. Use the transparency card to examine visibility through the dispersion. For a comparison at equal material loading, choose whether concentrations are matched by particle number or by material mass; these are different experiments. A 1 mm cuvette and a 10 mm cuvette give different optical depths even at the same concentration. The family photo places back- and front-lit views together so that the same samples can be compared under both conditions.

T7 · Distinguish coherent coupling from intensity transport

A sphere in a small cluster is excited by both the original illumination and the fields scattered by its neighbours. The T-matrix and chain lessons solve these coherent interactions between specified particles. Their fields retain phase. The macroscopic transport calculation in a disordered colloid instead follows the redistribution of intensity between scattering events. Both involve repeated scattering, but they describe different levels of the problem.

For a chain, compare longitudinal and transverse polarization at the same spacing, and compare the coupled result with the independent-sphere reference. Track a recognizable mode rather than whichever peak happens to be tallest. A size distribution or orientation average can combine several responses into a broad band; broadening alone does not establish a new loss process. A local electromagnetic model also ceases to be a complete description when a metal gap becomes small enough for tunnelling or charge transfer.

T8 · An inverse result is conditional on the model

Colour design searches candidate geometries and adjusts concentration to approach the selected target. There may be several satisfactory answers. A small colour difference is useful evidence about that observable, but says little by itself about robustness, chemical stability or whether another illumination would separate the candidates.

The same issue arises when fitting a measured spectrum. A changed radius, a distribution, an interfacial layer, additional damping or a changed refractive index can produce partly compensating effects. A hydrodynamic diameter includes solvated surface layers and need not equal a dense metal or silica-core diameter. Independent microscopy, composition or concentration measurements can therefore constrain an optical fit much more usefully than adding further free optical parameters.

Read a Museum fit in three parts: what was taken from the paper, what was reconstructed from the figure, and what was fitted. A fitted concentration fixes amplitude and cannot then serve as an independent validation of that amplitude. Parameters at imposed bounds deserve particular attention. The comparison remains useful, but an improved residual is not proof that the historical implementation was wrong.

T9 · Optical data and size-dependent electron scattering

Mie theory is exact for its specified spherical, local-material boundary-value problem. It cannot repair inaccurate or inappropriate material inputs. Optical constants measured on a film can depend on preparation, roughness, grain boundaries and surface chemistry. Published datasets for nominally the same metal need not give identical nanoparticle spectra. The material notes identify the selected specimen and supported wavelength range.

The optional Size-dependent electron scattering correction is off by default. It keeps the tabulated dielectric function and replaces only an assumed free-electron Drude contribution with one having a larger collision width. In energy units the added width is ΔΓ = AℏvF/Leff. The app uses the mean-chord convention Leff = 4V/S. This gives 4a/3 for a sphere and 4(R³−r³)/[3(R²+r²)] for a metal shell, approaching twice the thickness of a thin shell.

A = 1 represents the chosen diffuse-boundary model; it is not a measured constant for every surface. A 5 nm radius Au sphere has an added width of about 0.14 eV at A = 1, compared with about 0.014 eV at 50 nm radius. Each member of a size distribution uses its own dimensions. A metal core and a metal shell are corrected separately, with independently scattering boundaries as an approximation; a real metal–metal interface may transmit electrons.

Default gold uses an equal-weight mean of the Olmon single-crystal and McPeak complex permittivities. Each endpoint is interpolated in wavelength before averaging; averaging n and κ would give a different result. The separate endpoints and the Babar–Weaver dataset remain available for comparison. Their differences describe a material-model sensitivity, rather than a statistical uncertainty band. Fontijn magnetite uses figure-digitized diagonal permittivity, interpolated in photon energy; it does not include the off-diagonal magneto-optical tensor.

The Drude decomposition is an additional assumption. Consensus gold and its endpoints use Olmon’s plasma energy 8.45 eV and relaxation time 14 fs. Other metal choices retain the parameters stated in their model notes; Pt and Pd use an effective single-band velocity estimate. Mg and Li use an effective near-infrared decomposition of their selected data. Such corrections are best used to test sensitivity unless the parameters are supported for the specimen. Radiative damping is already included in the electromagnetic solution and is not added again. At dimensions of a few nanometres, a local Drude correction does not replace a description of atomic structure, nonlocal response or quantum confinement.

Model references: Rakić et al., optical models (1998); Hu et al., measured plasmon linewidths and boundary scattering (2008); Ye et al., gold-shell geometry and experiment (2018).

Detailed equations, conventions and source tables

Start with a physical question

Decide what would be measured before changing a parameter. A detector looking at one angle measures a different quantity from an instrument collecting light over many directions. A transmission measurement and a photograph of a front-lit vial are different again. The same particle model can predict all of these, but its outputs must be combined with the appropriate concentration, illumination and observation geometry.

A useful route through the app is 00–01: build the field → 02–04: understand size and angle → 05: add real materials → 06: design a colour → 07–08: couple particles → 09: test a historical comparison. The Lightbox connects spectra and transport to appearance. Use each lesson’s Guide to make a prediction, perform a controlled comparison and explain an observation.

Before the equations: five distinctions
  1. Radius and diameter: d = 2a. For a core–shell particle, outer radius = core radius + shell thickness.
  2. Vacuum and medium wavelength: λ = λ₀/nmed. Size parameter uses the latter: x = 2πa/λ.
  3. Field and intensity: complex fields add before squaring; intensities add directly only for an incoherent average or orthogonal contributions under the appropriate integral.
  4. Cross section and efficiency: C has units of area; Q = C/(πa²) is dimensionless.
  5. Particle and suspension: a single-particle cross section becomes an attenuation coefficient only after multiplying by a number concentration.

The selected Engineering or Physics depiction changes the explanations and equations below. The reading topics at the end apply to both.

Conventions

Time dependence e−iωt. A homogeneous, isotropic, nonmagnetic sphere of radius a sits in a lossless medium. k = 2π/λ is the wavenumber in the medium, x = ka the size parameter and m = n + iκ the refractive index relative to the medium (κ ≥ 0 is absorption). Results depend only on x and m, so they rescale to any wavelength: a = xλ/(2π).

The incident plane wave travels along +z. Linear polarization is E ∥ x or E ∥ y; unpolarized light is the incoherent mean of the two. The reference scattering plane is xz (φ = 0). The 3D view is right-handed: z points right, x up and y towards the default camera.

Vector spherical wave functions

A spherical-wave expansion is a choice of basis for an electromagnetic field, analogous to a Fourier expansion of a signal. The angular functions specify directional structure; the radial functions specify how the field varies with distance. Keeping these roles separate explains why the same angular mode appears in the near- and far-field lessons.

Mo1n = cosφ πn zn θ̂ − sinφ τn zn φ̂
Ne1n = n(n+1) sinθ cosφ πn (zn/ρ) r̂ + cosφ τn (ρzn)′/ρ θ̂ − sinφ πn (ρzn)′/ρ φ̂

ρ = kr; πn = Pn1(cosθ)/sinθ and τn = dPn1/dθ (π1 = 1, τ1 = cosθ). zn = jn for regular waves (superscript 1) and hn(1) for outgoing waves (superscript 3). M is purely transverse (toroidal); N has a radial part (poloidal). In the far field an outgoing wave of order n has the angular shape of lesson 00.

Lesson 00 uses unit-normalized angular functions: Nnang = An[τn cosφ θ̂ − πn sinφ φ̂], Mnang = An[πn cosφ θ̂ − τn sinφ φ̂], An = √[(2n+1)/2π]/[n(n+1)], so ∫|F|²dΩ = Σ|c|². N1 + M1 cancels exactly at θ = 180° (π1 + τ1 = 0): the first Kerker condition.

Plane wave and scattered field

The incoming plane wave is expanded about the sphere centre. Regular radial functions remain finite there; outgoing functions represent radiation leaving the particle. Continuity of the tangential electric and magnetic fields at the surface determines the complex coefficients an and bn. Their magnitudes and phases both matter in a directional interference pattern.

Einc/E0 = Σ En [Mo1n(1) − i Ne1n(1)],  En = in(2n+1)/[n(n+1)]
Esca/E0 = Σ En [i an Ne1n(3) − bn Mo1n(3)]

Only azimuthal index 1 appears for x- or y-polarized light along z. Because jn(kr) is tiny until kr ≈ n, a series truncated at N reproduces the plane wave only inside kr ≲ N.

an = [mψn(mx)ψ′n(x) − ψn(x)ψ′n(mx)] / [mψn(mx)ξ′n(x) − ξn(x)ψ′n(mx)]
bn = [ψn(mx)ψ′n(x) − mψn(x)ψ′n(mx)] / [ψn(mx)ξ′n(x) − mξn(x)ψ′n(mx)]

ψn(ρ) = ρjn(ρ), ξn(ρ) = ρhn(1)(ρ). The coefficients follow from continuity of tangential E and H at r = a.

Field inside the particle

E1/E0 = Σ En [cn Mo1n(1)(mkr) − i dn Ne1n(1)(mkr)],  cn = im/[ψn(mx)ξ′n(x) − mξn(x)ψ′n(mx)],  dn = im/[mψn(mx)ξ′n(x) − ξn(x)ψ′n(mx)]

The interior uses regular waves of argument mkr, so the local wavelength shrinks to λ/n and the field amplitude has a 1/e penetration depth λ/(2πκ), while intensity has a 1/e depth λ/(4πκ), using the wavelength and index convention of this section. The code evaluates cnψn(mkr) as a ratio ψn(mkr)/ψn(mx), which stays bounded even for strongly absorbing spheres. Tangential E is continuous at the surface and the normal component jumps by m², which the tests check point by point. With Add incident wave off the map shows E − Einc everywhere, which inside the particle is the change of the field caused by the particle.

Core–shell spheres

One interface is replaced by two. The core field must remain finite at its centre; the shell needs two independent radial solutions because it occupies an annulus. Matching both interfaces couples the optical response of the inner and outer surfaces. A thin metal shell can therefore have a resonance very different from a solid sphere with the same outer radius.

A concentric coated sphere (A. L. Aden & M. Kerker, J. Appl. Phys. 22, 1242 (1951)) has core radius ac, outer radius a, core index m1 and shell index m2 (both relative to the medium). In the shell the field needs both ψn and ξn of m2kr. Matching tangential E and H at both interfaces gives, per order, the scattering coefficients

an = [(Ha/m2 + n/x)ψn(x) − ψn−1(x)] / [(Ha/m2 + n/x)ξn(x) − ξn−1(x)],  bn: Ha/m2 → m2Hb

where Ha,b are effective logarithmic derivatives of the shell field at the outer surface (Yang, Appl. Opt. 42, 1710 (2003)). They are built only from logarithmic derivatives and bounded function ratios, so thick absorbing shells are stable. The coefficients agree with Bohren & Huffman's explicit formulas (sec. 8.1) to 10⁻⁹. Small coated spheres follow the quasi-static polarizability (B&H eq. 5.36).

Units, spectra and cross sections

In Fixed λ and Spectral modes the vacuum wavelength λ₀ (300–2000 nm), the radius and the materials are physical. The app uses x = 2πnmeda/λ₀ and m = Nparticle(λ₀)/nmed(λ₀). The medium is taken as lossless (its small absorption, e.g. water near 1450 and 1940 nm, is neglected). The spectral range can be narrowed anywhere inside 300–2000 nm (the range of the material data); the spectrum is always sampled at about 400 wavelengths, so a narrow range is resolved more finely. Sizes can be entered as radius or diameter; a core–shell particle is set by its core size and shell thickness. Absolute cross sections are C = Q πa² (a the outer radius) and the absolute differential cross section is dσ/dΩ = |S|²/k² with k = 2πnmed/λ₀.

Far field and cross sections

Far from the sphere, the angular amplitudes S₁ and S₂ separate from the outgoing radial factor. Squared amplitudes describe directional power, whereas integrating over direction gives a cross section. Do not compare the height of a normalized polar lobe with an integrated efficiency without accounting for that distinction.

S1(θ) = Σ (2n+1)/[n(n+1)] (anπn + bnτn),  S2(θ) = Σ (2n+1)/[n(n+1)] (anτn + bnπn)
I⊥ = |S1|²,  I∥ = |S2|²,  dσ/dΩ = |S|²/k²

For E ∥ x the scattered intensity in direction (θ, φ) is |S2|² cos²φ + |S1|² sin²φ. In the xz plane this is I∥ (E in the scattering plane); in the yz plane it is I⊥.

Qsca = (2/x²) Σ (2n+1)(|an|² + |bn|²),  Qext = (2/x²) Σ (2n+1) Re(an + bn) = (4/x²) Re S(0)
Qabs = Qext − Qsca,  g = ⟨cosθ⟩,  C = Q πa²

Integrated powers add order by order (orthogonality); amplitudes in a given direction interfere. The extinction formula is the optical theorem.

Limits

Rayleigh (x ≪ 1, |m|x ≪ 1). Only a1 ≈ −(2i/3) L x³ matters, L = (m²−1)/(m²+2):

Qsca ≈ (8/3) x⁴ |L|²,  Qabs ≈ 4x Im L,  I∥ ∝ cos²θ,  I⊥ ∝ 1,  Iunpol ∝ (1 + cos²θ)/2

So Csca ∝ a⁶/λ⁴, and light scattered at 90° is fully polarized. Absorption scales only as x, so absorbing particles are absorption-dominated when small. L diverges at m² = −2 (localized plasmon resonance).

Large spheres. Wiscombe's estimate N ≈ x + 4x1/3 + 2 orders are needed; |an| and |bn| drop sharply for n > x. The forward peak approaches Fraunhofer diffraction by a disc of the same radius, S ≈ x²(1+cosθ)/2 · J1(x sinθ)/(x sinθ), with its first zero at sinθ = 3.83/x (the principle of laser-diffraction sizing). Qext → 2 (extinction paradox: diffraction removes as much as the geometric shadow). For 1 < m < 2 the first-order Descartes rainbow appears at θ = π + 2i − 4r with cos²i = (m²−1)/3, sin i = m sin r.

Colour of a colloid and inverse design (lesson 06 and colour swatch)

The colour of a dispersion in a cuvette of path L follows from the extinction coefficient μext(λ) = N Cext(λ) = 0.75 φ Qext(λ)/a (volume fraction φ, outer radius a). In the single-scattering limit the transmitted fraction is T(λ) = exp(−μext L) (Beer–Lambert). Tristimulus values are X = Σ x̄(λ) Ssource(λ) T(λ)/Σ ȳ Ssource (and Y, Z alike) with the CIE 2006 10° colour-matching functions (CIE 170-2, tabulated by CVRL) and the selected illuminant, computed on a 10 nm grid with exact weights from the 1 nm tables. They are converted to CIE 1976 L*a*b* with the selected source white of the same observer; for display, XYZ is scaled to the sRGB white and converted to sRGB.

ΔE76 = [(ΔL*)² + (Δa*)² + (Δb*)²]1/2,   ΔE00: CIEDE2000 (lightness, chroma and hue weighting, blue rotation term)

Multiple scattering (colour swatch): Monte Carlo radiative transfer in a 1 cm × 1 cm cell with μs = N Csca, μa = N Cabs and the full Mie phase function; transmitted = light leaving the front face, scattered = light leaving the sides. Independent scattering is assumed (φ ≲ 10−3).

Inverse design (lesson 06), following Bleyer et al., J. Colloid Interface Sci. 691, 137408 (2025): for every candidate structure (Au, Ag, Cu, Si spheres; SiO₂@Au, SiO₂@Ag, Au@Ag, Ag@Au core–shell particles) the concentration minimizing ΔE to the target is found by a scan in log φ (10−8–10−3) and golden-section refinement. Grids of 64 diameters or 40 × 40 core diameters × shell thicknesses give the colour maps; the best design of each family is refined with Nelder–Mead in (sizes, log φ). The score is ΔE plus a small, displayed practicality penalty (core–shell synthesis step, shells < 5 nm, d > 250 nm, oxidizing copper, concentration at the range edge). Finalists are checked with Monte Carlo transport (re-ranked if ΔE00 changes by more than 2) and for robustness against a ±10 % size spread. Because three colour coordinates are matched with three parameters (two sizes and φ), core–shell solutions are often degenerate: a valley of equally good designs.

T-matrix and multiple scattering (lessons 07, 08)

The single-sphere solution maps incoming multipoles to outgoing ones. For neighbouring spheres, the outgoing field of one must be re-expanded as incoming regular waves at another. This translation is what allows particle geometry and coherent repeated interactions to enter the same basis used in lessons 00–03.

The chain lessons use normalized vector spherical wave functions of all azimuthal orders m (the eimφ dependence; in this section m is this index, not the relative refractive index), Mnm = ∇×(rψnm)/√(n(n+1)) and Nnm = ∇×Mnm/k with ψnm = zn(kr) Ynm(θ, φ). With incident (regular) coefficients a and scattered (outgoing) coefficients b about one origin, the T-matrix is defined by

b = T a,   sphere at the origin: T diagonal, bMnm = −bn aMnm,  bNnm = −an aNnm

For spheres at rj each is excited by the incident wave and by the waves scattered by all others, re-expanded about rj with the addition theorem (translation matrix H, which mixes M and N):

bj = Tj [aj + Σl≠j H(rj − rl) bl]  ⇒  b = (I − T H)−1 T a = ΣK≥0 (T H)K T a

The series (orders of scattering) converges only if the spectral radius ρ(T H) < 1. For spheres on a common axis z′ the translations conserve m, so the system splits into independent blocks; chains along x, y or z are solved in their own frame and the incident wave and all results are rotated. Cross sections follow without a far-field integral, Cext = −k−2 Re Σj aj*·bj, with the absorption of each sphere from bj and Tj, and Csca = Cext − Cabs. Efficiencies are given per sphere, Q = C/(N πa²). The T-matrix of the whole chain about an origin O, TO = Σjl J(O←j) [(I − T H)−1T]jl J(l←O), depends on O; the physics does not. Random orientation is averaged over the angle between k and the chain axis (Gauss–Legendre in cos β) and two polarizations.

Numerics. Regular translation coefficients by the recurrences that follow from the translation invariance of ∂/∂z and ∂/∂x + i∂/∂y; outgoing-to-regular coefficients by Gauss–Legendre projection on a sphere between the centres (the recurrence seeded with hn is unstable at high orders). Unknowns are scaled by |hn(x)|. Orders per sphere n ≤ x + 4x1/3 + 2 + ⌈3/√(g/a)⌉ for gap g. Checked against treams (cross sections and orientation averages agree to 3 × 10−8 at equal truncation), the single-sphere limit, energy conservation and the boundary conditions at every sphere surface. References: P. C. Waterman, Phys. Rev. D 3, 825 (1971); D. W. Mackowski, Proc. R. Soc. Lond. A 433, 599 (1991); W. C. Chew, J. Electromagn. Waves Appl. 6, 133 (1992); M. I. Mishchenko, L. D. Travis, A. A. Lacis, Scattering, Absorption, and Emission of Light by Small Particles (Cambridge, 2002); D. Beutel, I. Fernandez-Corbaton, C. Rockstuhl, treams, Comput. Phys. Commun. (2024).

How the display works

Far field: the radius of the 3D surface is I/Imax (linear) or max[0, 1 + dB/D] on a log radius scale with the selected range D = 40, 60 or 80 dB. Colour follows the chosen intensity mapping. It is a polar diagram of angular intensity, not a wavefront. Near field: the complete electric field on a plane through the centre, outside and inside the particle. Colours are scaled to the 99.5th percentile so a single hot spot does not wash out the map; arrows show Re E at the current time. Unpolarized light is an incoherent mixture of E ∥ x and E ∥ y with a random relative phase, so no instantaneous field Re E exists; only time-averaged intensities are defined. The map then shows |E|² (always ≥ 0, heat colours) or the signed intensity change |E|²/|E₀|² − 1 (blue: less light than incident, red: more), which is defined for every polarization and uses the total field. The grid adapts to at least 7 samples per wavelength (up to 321 × 321 points); when that is not enough a warning appears, and near-field maps are offered only for x ≤ 60.

Graphs: Cartesian angular graphs use |S|² = k² dσ/dΩ or absolute differential cross sections. Polar graphs use a shared maximum for normalization, as stated in their captions. Dashed lines show the full solution whenever the 3D view shows a partial sum.

Numerics and validation

Logarithmic derivatives Dn(mx) by downward recurrence; jn by series, Miller downward recurrence or upward recurrence as appropriate; yn upward. The bundled tests compare the coefficients with direct SciPy special functions (small, large, real, absorbing and high-index cases), check energy conservation, plane-wave reconstruction, the Rayleigh and Fraunhofer limits, the far-field limit and the angular integral of Qsca. Interior fields and coated spheres are checked against direct SciPy/Bohren–Huffman evaluations and against the boundary conditions at each interface. Live range: 0.01 ≤ x ≤ 200, relative n up to 6 and κ up to 25 (metals in the near infrared); near-field maps for x ≤ 60.

What the app calculates

A light wave meets a single spherical particle in a transparent medium such as air or water. Part of the light is absorbed, which means it is turned into heat. Another part is scattered into other directions. Mie theory is the exact solution of the equations of electromagnetism (Maxwell's equations) for this situation. The app solves these equations numerically, so its results approach the ideal-sphere solution as numerical truncation and sampling errors are controlled.

Only two numbers decide the result:

size parameter x = 2πa/λ = πd/λ   (a: radius, d: diameter, λ: wavelength in the medium)
relative refractive index m = nparticle/nmedium = n + iκ

x is the circumference of the particle divided by the wavelength. The real part n says how much more slowly light travels in the particle than in the medium. The imaginary part κ says how strongly the particle absorbs (κ = 0 means it is transparent). Only x and m matter. So a 100 nm particle at 400 nm behaves exactly like a 500 nm particle at 2000 nm, provided m is the same.

Cross sections and efficiencies

The cross section C is the area of the incoming beam whose power the particle removes. It can be larger or smaller than the particle's own cross-sectional area πa². Dividing by that area gives the dimensionless efficiency Q = C/(πa²).

extinction = scattering + absorption:  Cext = Csca + Cabs,  Qext = Qsca + Qabs

A dilute suspension has Np particles per unit volume. The fraction of light it transmits over a path length L follows the Beer–Lambert law, T = exp(−NpCextL). The product μext = NpCext is the extinction coefficient (often called turbidity); optical depth is τ = μextL. The asymmetry parameter g is the average cosine of the scattering angle. g = 0 means that the intensity-weighted mean of cosθ is zero; it does not require an isotropic pattern. As g → 1, scattering is concentrated towards the forward direction. g is a key input for models of light transport in coatings, foams, aerosols and photoreactors.

Where the light goes

The angular graphs show how much light is scattered in each direction θ. The angle 0° is straight ahead and 180° points back towards the light source. The graphs show the horizontal plane that contains the beam. Light whose electric field oscillates in this plane (∥) is scattered differently from light whose field is perpendicular to it (⊥). This is why scattered light is often partly polarized.

The plotted |S|² is proportional to the differential cross section dσ/dΩ = |S|²/k², with k = 2π/λ. This is the scattered power per unit solid angle. With Cross section C selected, it is shown in nm²/sr or µm²/sr.

The 3D far-field surface shows the same information in all directions. Its distance from the centre is the scattered intensity in that direction, or its level in decibels (every 10 dB is a factor of 10).

How the solution is built

Mie theory writes both the incoming and the scattered light as a sum of standard spherical wave patterns, much as a Fourier series writes a signal as a sum of sines and cosines (lessons 00 and 01). Each term has an order n = 1, 2, 3, … (dipole, quadrupole, octupole, …). Each order comes in two kinds, electric (coefficient an) and magnetic (coefficient bn). The coefficients follow from the requirement that the fields inside and outside the particle match at its surface.

Qsca = (2/x²) Σ (2n+1)(|an|² + |bn|²)

The total scattered power is a plain sum over the terms (graph Multipoles). In any one direction, however, the waves of the different terms interfere, and this creates the lobes of the angular pattern. About N ≈ x + 4x1/3 + 2 terms are needed. A calculation with fewer terms is incomplete, and the app marks it as a partial sum.

Size regimes and rules of thumb

Small particles, x ≪ 1 and |m|x ≪ 1 (Rayleigh scattering). The particle acts like a tiny antenna: an electric dipole driven by the incoming field.

Qsca ≈ (8/3) x⁴ |(m²−1)/(m²+2)|²,   Qabs ≈ 4x · Im[(m²−1)/(m²+2)]

So scattering grows as Csca ∝ a⁶/λ⁴. Halving the wavelength gives 16 times more scattering, which is why the sky and fine suspensions look bluish. Doubling the particle size gives 64 times more scattering. Absorption grows only as a³, so small absorbing particles mainly absorb. Light scattered at 90° is fully polarized.

Intermediate particles, x ≈ 1–50 (Mie regime). Scattering is strongly forward, with side lobes and resonances. Qext oscillates with size and, for glass-like particles, peaks near 4.

Large particles, x ≳ 50. Here Qext → 2. The particle blocks the light falling on its own cross-sectional area, and it diffracts the same amount again into a narrow forward cone. The first dark ring of this diffraction pattern lies at sin θ ≈ 3.83/x, which is the principle of particle sizing by laser diffraction. Away from the forward direction, ray optics applies: reflection, refraction and, for water droplets, the rainbow at about 138°.

Materials, colours and spectra

The refractive index of real materials depends on the wavelength. In the Fixed λ and Spectral modes the app uses measured data between 300 and 2000 nm (sources below). From the vacuum wavelength λ₀ it computes x = 2πnmediuma/λ₀ and m. Typical behaviour:

  • Metals (gold, silver, copper, aluminium): their free electrons can oscillate together, which is called a plasmon. Small metal particles absorb strongly where the real part of the relative permittivity ε = m² is about −2. This is near 520 nm for gold, which gives red colloids, and near 400 nm for silver, which gives yellow colloids. Size, a shell or the surrounding medium shift this resonance.
  • High-index materials (TiO₂, Si, ZnO) scatter strongly with little loss. TiO₂ makes white paints opaque. Silicon nanoparticles show an additional magnetic resonance, caused by light circulating inside them.
  • Polymers, silica and water scatter weakly in water because m is close to 1. They absorb only in specific bands, for example water near 1450 and 1940 nm.

A core–shell particle combines two materials, for example a silica core with a thin gold shell. The shell thickness tunes the resonance. The medium is treated as non-absorbing.

Fields near and inside the particle

The Near field view shows the electric field on a plane through the particle centre, both inside and outside the particle.

  • Re E (snapshot) is the oscillating field at one instant; the animation runs through one optical cycle.
  • |E|² is the time-averaged intensity. This is what a detector or a light-driven reaction responds to.
  • The intensity change |E|²/|E₀|² − 1 shows where the particle concentrates light (red: hot spots, for example behind a sphere or next to a metal particle) and where it removes light (blue: shadow).

Unpolarized light has no single instantaneous field, so only intensity maps are shown for it.

The colour of a colloid, and designing for a colour

A colloid looks coloured because its particles remove some wavelengths of the light passing through more than others. The app computes how much of each wavelength gets through 1 cm of the sample at a given concentration, weights it with the sensitivity of the eye and the spectrum of the selected light source (international CIE observer conventions), and turns that into a colour. Colours are compared with the standard colour difference ΔE: smaller values indicate a closer match under the chosen observer and illumination. Visibility also depends on lightness, surroundings and the conditions of comparison.

Lesson 06 turns this round: you choose a colour, and the app tries thousands of particle designs (gold, silver, copper and silicon spheres, and silica or metal cores with a metal shell) at all sensible concentrations and picks the one that comes closest. Simpler and more robust designs win near-ties. The best designs are then re-checked with a full simulation of light bouncing between many particles. The approach follows Bleyer et al. (2025), FAU.

Several particles: the T-matrix

The T-matrix is a table that describes how a particle turns any incoming wave pattern into scattered patterns: scattered = T × incoming. For one sphere at the centre the table only has diagonal entries, the Mie coefficients. Once the T-matrix of a particle is known, its scattering can be calculated for every direction and polarization of the light.

When several particles are close together, each one is lit by the incoming light and by the light scattered by its neighbours. Writing this down for every particle gives a set of linear equations:

scattered by particle j = Tj × [incoming at j + light arriving from all other particles]

Solving them step by step (exchange after exchange, lesson 07) works when the particles interact weakly. When they interact strongly, as metal spheres a few nanometres apart, the steps run away and the equations must be solved in one go, which is what the app does. Efficiencies are given per sphere, Q = C/(N πa²), so a chain can be compared directly with one sphere. Random orientation averages over all directions of the light, as for particles in a stirred suspension.

The calculation was checked against the independent T-matrix code treams; the results agree to better than one part in ten million.

Where this is used

  • Particle sizing by laser diffraction (ISO 13320) and by static light scattering.
  • Turbidity and extinction of suspensions, emulsions and aerosols; the colour of colloids.
  • Pigments, coatings and opacity (TiO₂, CaCO₃); the appearance of foams and emulsions.
  • Light management in solar cells and photocatalytic reactors; plasmonic sensors and photothermal heating.
  • Aggregation of colloids, nanoparticle chains and assemblies, and the strong local fields used in surface-enhanced spectroscopy (lessons 07 and 08).

From a particle size to a measured quantity

The size parameter compares the circumference of a particle with the wavelength in its surroundings. For a 100 nm diameter sphere in water at vacuum wavelength 600 nm, taking nmed = 1.33 gives λ ≈ 451 nm and x ≈ 0.696. The same physical particle has a different x in air. The relative index m = (np + iκp)/nmed also changes when the medium changes. Altering the medium therefore changes both wave size and optical contrast.

For an incident irradiance I₀, a cross section converts to power through Psca = I₀Csca, with the analogous expression for absorption. An efficiency greater than one is allowed: the cross section is an effective beam area, not a probability that a photon striking the geometrical disc is scattered. For passive particles in a lossless medium, Cext = Csca + Cabs.

A directional quantity needs a solid angle. In the far field, a small detector of area Ad at distance r subtends approximately ΔΩ = Ad/r². It receives P ≈ I₀(dσ/dΩ)ΔΩ if the intensity is nearly constant across that aperture. Integrating dσ/dΩ over 4π gives Csca. A large detector can average over narrow fringes even when the calculation resolves them.

The asymmetry parameter g = ⟨cosθ⟩ is an intensity-weighted angular average. Positive g indicates a bias towards forward directions. g = 0 means zero mean cosine; it does not imply isotropy or equal integrated forward and backward powers for every possible pattern. Different angular distributions can share the same g.

Concentration example. For monodisperse spheres, number concentration Np = φ/(4πa³/3), where φ is particle volume fraction. The extinction coefficient μext = NpCext has units of inverse length. Optical depth τ = μextL is dimensionless and unscattered transmission is exp(−τ). Spectrophotometer absorbance is A = −log₁₀T = τ/ln 10 for that measurement model; extinction in an absorbing and scattering sample is not absorption alone.

Check. In a nonabsorbing Rayleigh suspension, Csca ∝ a⁶. At fixed number concentration, doubling a raises μsca by 64. At fixed volume fraction, the number concentration falls by eight, so μsca rises by eight. State which concentration is held fixed whenever comparing particle sizes.

Reading polar diagrams and the 3D far field

θ is measured from the incident propagation direction: 0° is forward, 90° is side scattering and 180° is backward. φ rotates around the beam. The reference xz scattering plane contains the beam and the x axis. I∥ and I⊥ label the electric field relative to that plane. Unpolarized intensity is the incoherent mean (I∥ + I⊥)/2, rather than the squared field of their sum.

A polar diagram uses distance from its centre to represent relative intensity. Its two halves repeat the angular dependence in the scattering plane; they are not two different experiments. A 3D pattern extends the representation over azimuth. The radius of either diagram is a plotting convention and is not the physical distance travelled by light or the shape of a wavefront. Polar curves share a common maximum for comparison; a normalized shape alone cannot tell you the absolute scattered power.

level in dB = 10 log₁₀(I/Imax)
−20 dB: 1/100 of maximum intensity · −40 dB: 1/10 000 · −60 dB: 1/1 000 000

On a linear radial scale, weak features lie close to the centre. On the 3D log scale, displayed radius is R₀ max[0, 1 + (level in dB)/D], where D is the selected 40, 60 or 80 dB range. Increasing D reveals weaker levels but compresses the radial separation between levels already visible. Log radius is a display transformation; it does not amplify the physical scattered power.

Explore a small feature. In a polar plot, wheel or pinch around the feature to magnify it, then drag to pan. The +/− buttons and keyboard equivalents also work; Reset or Home restores the full diagram. The legend stays fixed, and detector angles are calculated from the transformed diagram. Zoom is preserved while a parameter changes and resets on changing lessons or graph type.

In 3D, use the far-field +/− buttons, wheel or pinch for up to 80× magnification. Shift-drag or two-finger drag pans; ordinary drag rotates. The camera magnifies its projection rather than crossing into the intensity surface. Focus detector direction centres the view on the direction chosen by the detector controls; Reset camera returns to the full pattern. Camera magnification and the polar-plot zoom are independent.

Rainbow investigation. In Large spheres, use the guided water-droplet setup at x = 200. Keep all multipoles, use a 60 dB radius scale, and hide the particle. Inspect the region around θ ≈ 138° and the neighbouring oscillations. Ray optics predicts the primary rainbow caustic; the exact wave solution gives a finite maximum and interference structure. A single-wavelength false-colour plot is not a photograph of a coloured rainbow. B&H §7.2, pp. 174–177, explains the angular geometry.

Angular sampling also matters. The single-sphere 3D surface first draws a coarse view, then refines to as many as 4800 θ intervals at x = 200. Magnification does not create information beyond the numerical grid. Confirm a very fine feature in the angular graph, and distinguish a genuine oscillation from an under-resolved edge or display interpolation.

Near field is a different measurement. A map of |E|² shows electric-field enhancement. Close to a particle it is not, by itself, a map of net energy flow: the time-averaged Poynting vector is ½ Re(E × H*), involving both fields and their relative phase. A bright electric-field hot spot and a strong far-field scattering direction therefore need not coincide. B&H §2.5, pp. 23–25, introduces this distinction.

What a size distribution changes

A real sample contains a population, not a single representative sphere. The Real particles and Sphere chains lessons can average over log-normal or positive Gaussian size distributions. The size control specifies an arithmetic mean; the relative spread is the coefficient of variation, CV = standard deviation/mean. A log-normal distribution is positive and skewed: its mean, median and most probable size are different.

For a log-normal radius: σ²ln a = ln(1 + CV²),   μln a = ln⟨a⟩ − σ²ln a/2
μext = Np⟨Cext⟩,   φ = Np⟨V⟩

The average is over number fractions: calculate the cross section of each sampled particle, then weight and sum. In general ⟨C(a)⟩ ≠ C(⟨a⟩), and ⟨a³⟩ ≠ ⟨a⟩³. Averaging efficiencies instead of cross sections gives a different answer unless the area weighting is accounted for. Likewise, number concentration at fixed volume fraction must use the mean particle volume. The app carries these moments through to colour and Lightbox concentration conversions.

For a coated particle, core size and shell thickness can have separate spreads. In the current population model they are independently sampled; that assumption does not describe every synthesis. A shared core batch across several samples should retain the same core distribution while shell means or spreads change. Within chains, sharing a sampled bead size and sampling beads independently describe different populations, so inspect the chosen bead-sampling option.

Size averaging can remove narrow ripple features and broaden spectral bands. It does not prove that an experimental width is caused only by polydispersity: shape, interfaces, optical constants, instrument resolution and aggregation can contribute too. Compare a single-size calculation, then vary one distribution at a time. B&H Fig. 11.6 (p. 298) provides a particularly useful example of size dispersion smoothing extinction structure.

From a spectrum to colour and a Lightbox image

A transmission colour follows the spectrum that reaches the observer, not the extinction spectrum plotted directly. In the unscattered limit, multiply the illuminant spectrum by exp[−μext(λ)L], then integrate with the colour-matching functions. A dip in green transmission can leave a red-looking gold colloid; the colour of scattered light can be different. Concentration and path length enter through optical depth, so neither is an optional detail of a colour specification.

The app offers D65 and three other source spectra. Changing the source changes the relative numbers of photons at different wavelengths. Source-white adaptation and fixed-white display are different choices: the former adapts the source white to the display white, whereas the latter retains a visible source tint. Two samples matching under one source can separate under another, a form of metamerism.

The Lightbox combines transmission, scattering and the glass container. It uses spectral transport with Mie extinction, scattering, absorption and angular phase functions; light can scatter repeatedly before leaving the sample. Glass boundaries introduce reflection and refraction. The curved wall, base and meniscus change the paths through the dispersion, so edges may show a different balance of transmitted and scattered colour from the centre. The transparency card makes this distinction easier to inspect.

A nearly clear, weakly scattering red colloid can preserve details behind the sample while tinting them. A larger or more strongly scattering dispersion adds light arriving from other directions and can obscure those details. Milky white front-lit polystyrene and warm-tinted transmission are therefore compatible: they correspond to different illumination and photon paths. The four Lightbox arrangements help separate these observations.

Fast previews prioritize an early whole-scene impression. Refined renders use the full transport calculation; exports wait for their requested final rendering and run from a snapshot of the sample and scene settings. A preview’s residual noise or temporary spectral colouring should not be interpreted as a physical feature. Even a converged image remains a model of an ideal container and prescribed lighting, rather than a calibrated prediction for every camera, exposure or laboratory room.

Inverse colour design. A small ΔE identifies a colour match under the chosen observer, source, adaptation and geometry. It does not guarantee identical spectra, an experimentally accessible synthesis, or an equally good front-lit match. Compare candidate spectra and vary concentration, distribution width and illumination to assess how robust the design is.

Choose the model before interpreting the result

Mie theory solves Maxwell’s equations for an ideal homogeneous sphere; the coated-sphere solution adds concentric interfaces. Numerical results still depend on retained multipoles, interpolation of material data and angular or spatial resolution. A converged calculation is a solution of those assumptions, not proof that a real sample satisfies them. Material sources and their wavelength ranges are listed below.

The surrounding medium is treated as lossless. Measured bulk optical constants need not describe a very thin metal shell or a few-nanometre particle. Independent-particle transport neglects correlations, dependent scattering and electromagnetic coupling between random close neighbours. The chain lessons include coherent coupling within a specified chain, but classical local electrodynamics does not include electron tunnelling across a subnanometre gap or a chemically altered interface.

There are two different uses of “multiple scattering” here. In the T-matrix lessons, waves are exchanged coherently among nearby spheres, retaining phase and near-field coupling. In the suspension swatches and Lightbox, radiative transfer follows intensity transport between independent particles using their scattering phase functions. These models apply at different structural scales and should not be interchanged.

A useful report states the particle geometry, size definition and distribution; material data and medium; illumination and polarization; concentration and optical path; plotted observable and normalization; and any convergence check. For a historical overlay, additionally distinguish the book’s settings from modern material data, fitted dimensions or chosen amplitude registration. Agreement can test a model; disagreement can expose several competing assumptions.
