About the solid model

The particles are dispersed throughout the solid walls, base and handle. The interior is empty air. Optical paths follow the supplied Humpen.stl surface, including the dimples. STL coordinates are interpreted as millimetres. All four Lightbox lighting arrangements remain available.

The selected matrix changes both refraction at the surface and the particles’ Mie response inside it. The calculation includes absorption and multiple scattering. It assumes an optically smoothed, homogeneous print. Actual layer boundaries, air voids, yellowing and surface roughness can make printed glass or polymers much hazier than this ideal material.

Narrow photographic front light replaces the broad frontal illumination by a small distant softbox. It has an approximately 16° angular full width at half maximum and comparable total frontal irradiance. This makes directional backscattering easier to see. It changes only Front light. The source spectrum is still selected above.

The logo is an opaque dark-blue surface print. It rotates with the object. The coloured floor shadow uses direct transmission through the mesh and does not include refractive caustic focusing. Up to three samples can be shown in this mode.

Polymer dispersion follows Sultanova et al. (2009). Their fit is measured from 436.8 nm. Its extension to 380 nm is a smooth extrapolation. The glass uses the SCHOTT N-BK7 Sellmeier formula. N-BK7 and the polymer matrices are treated as non-absorbing. The two soda–lime choices use Rubin’s measured glass absorption and dispersion (1985). They are modern window-glass proxies, not optical constants measured on the Lycurgus Cup. Transparent SiO₂ spheres can be added as a second scattering population. They model silica-rich heterogeneity with a uniform spatial distribution. This does not reconstruct an ancient glass microstructure. Ray directions use n at 550 nm, while the particle spectra use the dispersive host at each wavelength.

About the Lycurgus analogue

The Lycurgus effect: compare Back light with Front light, or open Family photo. The Ag₇₀Au₃₀ sample is close to the reported metal ratio, with the minor copper contribution omitted. The two Ag₈₀Au₂₀ samples were chosen for stronger colour contrast. They are optical analogues, not the measured composition of the cup. The clear and green soda–lime matrices are measured modern proxies. SiO₂ spheres stand in for silica-rich regions. The ancient glass also has a more complex microstructure. The reference binder is a transparent acrylic model with n = 1.49. Set another index to explore a different clear binder. The coloured particles retain their sizes and distributions, and their optical response is recalculated in this binder.

Viewing the film and reading the card

Light arrives at 45° and the film is viewed along its normal, away from the surface reflection. The neutral grey surround and D65 reference source follow the approach used for visual comparison of paint colours. Choose another source above to explore how the colour changes.

The cards use idealized black and white reflectances of 3% and 90%. The checkerboard combines both on the same card. C = Yblack / Ywhite compares the coated areas. As C approaches 1, the film hides the card. A lighter colour does not necessarily mean better hiding.

Both loading sliders refer to the volume of the dry film. The binder fills the remaining volume. TiO₂ adds 250 nm scattering spheres using the app’s measured Siefke optical constants. It is a model white pigment, rather than a particular commercial rutile grade.

A thick layer need not become black. Once the substrate is hidden, light scattered back near the surface still reaches the eye. Its colour depends on the balance of scattering and absorption. Small, absorption-dominated metal particles tend to give darker films than strongly scattering larger particles. At high metal fractions, plasmon coupling, aggregation and changes of the effective dielectric response can alter or broaden the colour. These concentration-dependent particle interactions are outside this independent-particle model.

The calculation follows light through a finite layer, including repeated scattering, substrate returns and reflection inside the binder surface. It uses the particles’ Mie spectra and angular scattering. It assumes separate spheres in a uniform binder. Crowding, aggregation, porosity and interference between neighbouring particles are omitted, so concentrated paint predictions are qualitative. The screen display is an educational comparison, rather than a certified colour or opacity measurement.
